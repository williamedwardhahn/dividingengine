# A Complete History of Computation — Master List (v0.1)

Purpose: seed content for a modular, HyperCard-style teaching site. Core thesis: **mathematics has been done by machines (and machine-like human systems) for millennia**. Students doing arithmetic by hand are practicing a living historical tradition — one that is now automated at nearly unimaginable speeds.

Structure: each entry below will become a "card." Cards link across eras and across the five cross-cutting threads at the end.

---

## Era 1 — Prehistoric & Tally Computation (c. 43,000 BCE – 3000 BCE)

- **c. 43,000 BCE — Lebombo bone** (Eswatini/South Africa): baboon fibula with 29 tally notches; possibly the oldest known mathematical artifact.
- **c. 20,000 BCE — Ishango bone** (Congo): notched bone with groupings suggesting counting, possibly doubling or prime awareness.
- **Tally sticks & body counting**: externalized memory — the first "storage devices." Counting offloaded from the mind onto an object.
- **c. 8000–3000 BCE — Mesopotamian clay tokens**: small shaped tokens representing goods; sealed in clay envelopes (bullae) — precursor to writing and to symbolic data.
- **Knotted-cord counting traditions** (worldwide; later formalized as the Inca quipu).

## Era 2 — Ancient Computation (c. 3000 BCE – 500 CE)

- **c. 3000 BCE — Sumerian sexagesimal (base-60) system**: still with us in minutes, seconds, degrees.
- **c. 2000 BCE — The edubba ("tablet house")** (Sumer): the first documented schools — students copy multiplication and reciprocal tables onto clay; surviving exercise tablets preserve their mistakes and doodles. Math homework is 4,000 years old, and it was table-copying.
- **c. 2500 BCE — Earliest abacus/counting boards** (Sumer): the first widely used calculating machine.
- **c. 1800 BCE — Babylonian mathematical tablets**: Plimpton 322 (Pythagorean triples), YBC 7289 (√2 to ~6 decimal digits) — precomputed tables as "software."
- **c. 1650 BCE — Rhind Mathematical Papyrus** (Egypt): algorithmic problem-solving, unit fractions, multiplication by doubling; opens by promising "the correct method of reckoning" — math as recipe.
- **c. 1500 BCE — Water clocks (clepsydrae)** (Egypt, then Babylon, Greece, China): time computed by flow; Ctesibius of Alexandria (c. 250 BCE) adds a feedback float valve — the most accurate timekeepers on Earth for ~3,000 years, until the pendulum.
- **c. 1300 BCE — Egyptian rope-stretchers (harpedonaptai)**: surveying teams re-measuring fields after each Nile flood — number as *length on the ground*; Greek tradition credits them as the origin of geometry ("earth-measure").
- **c. 1000–800 BCE — I Ching hexagrams** (China): 64 six-line figures built from broken/unbroken lines — a complete binary combinatorics; in 1701–03 Leibniz, shown the hexagrams, marveled that his binary arithmetic had been anticipated by millennia.
- **c. 800–500 BCE — Śulba Sūtras** (India): precise altar-construction geometry in the Vedic tradition; the Pythagorean rule stated and applied centuries before Pythagoras.
- **c. 500 BCE — The Pythagoreans**: "all is number" — reality as whole numbers and their ratios; figurate numbers arranged with pebbles (*calculi*, the root of "calculate").
- **c. 500 BCE — The monochord**: Pythagorean legend ties musical intervals to string ratios — 2:1 the octave, 3:2 the fifth — the first natural law stated in numbers; music becomes the founding proof that nature is mathematical (Thread T).
- **c. 480 BCE — Steganography enters history**: Herodotus records messages tattooed on a slave's shaved scalp and hidden under the wax of writing tablets (Demaratus warning Greece of Xerxes' invasion) — concealing the *existence* of a message, the twin art to cryptography (see Thread J).
- **458 BCE — The beacon relay**: Aeschylus' *Agamemnon* describes news of Troy's fall leaping mountain to mountain by fire in one night; Persia's Royal Road couriers and Rome's cursus publicus build the relay-post alternative — the dream of instant long-distance signaling is ancient (see Thread M).
- **c. 450–400 BCE — The incommensurability crisis**: the diagonal of a square has no whole-number ratio to its side (√2 is irrational); Greek thought splits *arithmos* (discrete number) from *megethos* (continuous magnitude).
- **c. 370 BCE — Eudoxus' theory of proportion**: rigorous treatment of irrational magnitudes — the ancestor of the real numbers, completed by Dedekind 2,200 years later.
- **c. 300 BCE — Salamis Tablet**: oldest surviving counting board (Greek).
- **c. 300 BCE — Euclid's Elements**: axiomatic method; Euclid's algorithm for GCD — an algorithm still taught and still running in computers today.
- **c. 295 BCE — The Library of Alexandria**: humanity's first attempt at total information storage and retrieval; Callimachus' *Pinakes* catalog is the ancestor of every index, card catalog, database, and search engine (Thread S).
- **c. 200 BCE onward — Chinese counting rods and the suanpan (abacus)**: fast positional arithmetic; skilled operators rival early electronic calculators.
- **c. 200 BCE – 200 CE — Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art** (China): algorithm-first mathematics — systems of linear equations solved by rod manipulation (essentially Gaussian elimination), negative numbers as red/black rods.
- **c. 36 BCE — Maya Long Count**: earliest dated zero in the Americas; base-20 numeration serving that civilization's central quantity — *time*.
- **c. 150–100 BCE — Antikythera mechanism**: geared analog computer predicting eclipses and planetary positions; ~30 bronze gears.
- **c. 60 CE — Hero of Alexandria**: programmable automata (pegged drums = stored program in embryo).
- **c. 150 CE — Ptolemy's Almagest**: massive trigonometric (chord) tables — centuries of "compute" frozen into a book.
- **Roman hand abacus**: pocket calculator of the ancient world.

## Era 3 — Zero, Algorithms & Medieval Machines (500 – 1500)

- **c. 520 — Boethius' *De institutione musica***: music sorted into *mundana* (the spheres), *humana* (body and soul), and *instrumentalis* (what we hear); his textbook fixes music inside the quadrivium beside arithmetic, geometry, and astronomy — for a thousand years, music class *was* math class (Thread T).
- **c. 628 — Brahmagupta** (India): rules for zero and negative numbers; zero as a number, not just a placeholder.
- **725 — Bede's finger reckoning**: *De computo vel loquela digitorum* — a standardized system for counting to 9,999 on two hands; monks compute the date of Easter (*computus* — the medieval word family that gives us "computer").
- **9th–12th c. — The dust board (takht)**: Islamic arithmeticians compute Hindu numerals on erasable sand boards — algorithms designed around *erasing as you go*, later adapted to pen and paper.
- **c. 820 — al-Khwarizmi** (House of Wisdom, Baghdad): *Al-jabr* (→ "algebra") and his text on Hindu numerals (→ "algorithm" from his Latinized name). Computation as rule-following procedure.
- **9th c. — Banu Musa brothers**: *Book of Ingenious Devices*, programmable machines and automata.
- **9th c. — al-Kindi**: *On Deciphering Cryptographic Messages* — frequency analysis; statistics as a computational weapon (see Cryptography thread).
- **1206 — al-Jazari**: elaborate programmable automata, including a programmable drum machine (camshaft "programming").
- **c. 1200s–1400s — Girih tiles** (Islamic world): five decorated tiles generating intricate quasi-periodic ornament — patterns on the Darb-i Imam shrine anticipate Penrose tilings by five centuries (recognized only in 2007).
- **c. 1280s–1300s — The mechanical clock & verge escapement** (Europe): gear trains that ration time into equal ticks — Europe's apprenticeship in precision gearing, without which no calculating machines; "clockwork" becomes the reigning metaphor for the universe itself.
- **c. 1280s — Abraham Abulafia's letter permutations**: ecstatic Kabbalah as combinatorial practice — Hebrew letters permuted in disciplined sequences as meditative technology, with gematria assigning numbers to words; devotion as symbol-manipulation, right beside Llull's wheels (Thread U).
- **1305 — Ramon Llull's Ars Magna** (Majorca): rotating paper wheels that combine concepts mechanically to generate arguments — the audacious claim that *reasoning itself* can be mechanized. Leibniz's own dissertation builds on Llull's combinatorics; he is the godfather of symbolic AI, seven centuries early.
- **1202 — Fibonacci's Liber Abaci**: Hindu-Arabic numerals enter European commerce; algorists vs. abacists — a centuries-long fight over *how* to compute.
- **13th–16th c. — Italian abbaco schools (scuole d'abbaco)**: merchant children (including Leonardo da Vinci and Machiavelli) trained in commercial arithmetic, lattice multiplication, and the Rule of Three — Europe's first mass mathematics education, driven by trade.
- **c. 1450 — Gutenberg's printing press**: mass replication of identical text and *identical numbers* — printed tables, textbooks, and almanacs make shared computation possible at scale; the first mass data-copying technology (Thread M).
- **1463 — Ficino translates the Corpus Hermeticum**: Cosimo de' Medici orders Plato set aside so Hermes Trismegistus can be translated *first* — the Renaissance reboots with the Emerald Tablet's "as above, so below" at the front of the queue (Thread U).
- **Medieval counting houses & jetons**: the "exchequer" is literally a checkered counting board — government by calculation.
- **c. 1400s — Inca quipu & yupana**: knotted-cord decimal data storage administering an empire without writing, paired with the yupana grid counting board — database and calculator, together.
- **Astrolabes & equatoria**: analog computers for astronomy carried in the hand.
- **c. 1025 — Guido of Arezzo**: the musical staff — pitch plotted against time, arguably the first graph, six centuries before Descartes' coordinates; solmization (do–re–mi) and the Guidonian hand turn a monk's palm into memory hardware (Thread T).
- **1088 — Su Song's astronomical clock tower** (China): a 12-meter water-driven celestial computer — escapement, rotating armillary sphere, and time-announcing manikins.
- **8th–15th c. — Zij tables** (Islamic world): vast computed astronomical handbooks; al-Kashi (Samarkand, 1424) calculates π to 16 decimal places and perfects decimal fractions — a precision record standing nearly two centuries.
- **c. 1400 — The Kerala school** (Madhava of Sangamagrama, India): infinite series for π, sine, and cosine — power-series results two centuries before Newton and Leibniz.

## Era 4 — The Calculating Revolution (1500 – 1800)

- **1494 — Luca Pacioli codifies double-entry bookkeeping**: debits must equal credits — a built-in checksum for commerce, and the information system that will finance everything else on this timeline (Thread R).
- **1522 — Adam Ries**: German *Rechenmeister* whose vernacular arithmetic textbooks teach a nation to calculate — "nach Adam Riese" ("per Adam Ries") still means "correctly computed" in German today.
- **c. 1540s — Codex Vergara** (Aztec/Nahua): surveyors' records of chinampa field areas using indigenous fractional units (arrows, hearts, hands) — sophisticated land-area computation running in parallel with, and invisible to, European mathematics.
- **1557 — Robert Recorde invents "="**: English math textbooks written as teacher–student dialogues; the equals sign born of a teacher's impatience with writing "is equal to" over and over.
- **1564/1570 — John Dee**: the *Monas Hieroglyphica* compresses the cosmos into a single occult glyph; six years later his Mathematical Preface to the first English Euclid preaches mathematics' power to navigators, artisans, and engineers — the magus as math evangelist, one mind holding both worlds (Thread U).
- **1580s — Prosthaphaeresis**: astronomers (Wittich, Clavius; used in Tycho Brahe's observatory) convert multiplication into addition via trigonometric identities — the trick logarithms would soon make obsolete.
- **1614 — John Napier: logarithms**: multiplication converted into addition — arguably the greatest "computational speedup" before electronics.
- **1614 — Casaubon dates the Hermetica**: philology proves the "primordially ancient" Hermetic texts are late-antique — Hermes' stock falls in the very year Napier's logarithms appear; one revolution demotes magic while another accelerates mathematics (Thread U).
- **1617 — Napier's bones**: multiplication rods for laypeople.
- **1617–1624 — Henry Briggs: base-10 logarithm tables**: computed by hand to 14 decimal places; log tables become the working engine of science and engineering for the next 350 years — schoolchildren still carried them in the 1970s.
- **c. 1600s — Japanese soroban**: refined 1/4-bead abacus; in a famous 1946 Tokyo contest a soroban operator beat a US Army electric calculator in four of five rounds.
- **1597 — Galileo's geometric and military compass (the sector)**: a hinged brass calculating instrument for gunners and surveyors; Galileo manufactured and sold them with lessons — a scientist funding research by selling calculators.
- **1619 — Kepler's *Harmonices Mundi***: hunting the chords of the planets, he finds the third law of planetary motion — music-of-the-spheres mysticism delivering hard celestial mechanics (Thread T).
- **1620 — Gunter's scale**: logarithms engraved on wood, worked with dividers — the missing link between Napier's tables and the slide rule.
- **c. 1622 — William Oughtred: slide rule**: the engineer's computer for the next 350 years (Apollo astronauts carried them).
- **1623 — Wilhelm Schickard's "calculating clock"**: first known mechanical calculator (destroyed by fire; long forgotten).
- **1636 — Mersenne's *Harmonie universelle***: pitch measured as countable vibrations per second (by watching the slow swings of very long strings, then scaling up by law); the string laws — frequency ∝ (1/L)·√(T/μ) — from varying one parameter at a time; equal temperament's ¹²√2 ≈ 1.05946 computed and printed as fret lengths; and all 720 permutations of a six-note hexachord tabulated, with 22! melodies declared inexhaustible. Acoustics founded, Western music retuned, and combinatorics applied to art — in one 1,500-page book (Thread T).
- **1642 — Blaise Pascal: the Pascaline**: mechanical addition/subtraction with carry — built at age 19 for his tax-collector father.
- **1650 — Kircher's *Musurgia universalis* and arca musarithmica**: birdsong transcribed into musical notation, and a box that composes music by combinatorial lookup — an algorithmic composition machine (Thread T).
- **1641–1669 — Kircher's engines of everything**: the magic lantern popularized (projected images — ancestor of every screen), museum statues that speak through hidden voice tubes, a universal cipher-language with a translation box (*Polygraphia nova*, 1663), Llull's combinatorics rebuilt as a general logic of discovery (*Ars Magna Sciendi*, 1669), and a worldwide Jesuit network mailing him magnetic and tide observations — a one-man platform era, three centuries early (Thread U).
- **1673 — Gottfried Leibniz: stepped reckoner**: all four operations; Leibniz also formalizes **binary arithmetic (1703)** and dreams of mechanized reasoning ("Calculemus!").
- **1668–77 — Change ringing formalized** (Fabian Stedman, England): bell-ringers systematically enumerate permutations — working group theory in church towers, 150 years before mathematicians name it.
- **1668 — Wilkins's *Real Character***: a constructed philosophical language classifying every concept — the Babel-reversing project that Leibniz would turn computational (Thread T).
- **1701 — Sauveur names "acoustics"**: harmonics identified, named, and measured — Mersenne's puzzle of one string sounding many pitches at once begins to yield (Thread T).
- **1701–03 — Leibniz, Bouvet, and the binary God**: Jesuit missionary Joachim Bouvet mails Leibniz the I Ching's hexagram circle; Leibniz reads the 64 figures as his own binary arithmetic and publishes the connection — for him binary was theology, 1 as God and 0 as nothing, creation *ex nihilo* inside every number; he even designed a commemorative medallion of it (Era 2; Thread U).
- **c. 1680s–1727 — Newton the alchemist**: translator of the Emerald Tablet into English, author of roughly a million manuscript words on alchemy (more than on physics), and computer of biblical chronologies — including a calculation that the apocalypse comes "not before 2060." When Keynes bought the long-hidden papers at auction in 1936, he concluded Newton was "not the first of the age of reason" but "the last of the magicians" (Thread U).
- **1683 — Seki Takakazu and wasan** (Japan): determinants before Leibniz publishes them, Bernoulli numbers before Bernoulli — an independent Japanese mathematical tradition whose devotees hang **sangaku**, carved geometry problems, as offerings in shrines and temples.
- **1582 — The Gregorian calendar reform**: Clavius' commission computes away accumulated drift — Thursday, October 4 is followed by Friday, October 15. Arithmetic edits reality's date.
- **1591–1637 — Modern notation is invented**: Viète's letters for unknowns, then Descartes' x, y, z and exponents; +, −, ×, and Recorde's = spread in the same era — algebraic symbolism as a compact machine language for *human* computation.
- **1654 — Pascal & Fermat's correspondence**: a gambler's dispute births probability theory — chance becomes calculable (Thread P).
- **1662/1693 — Graunt's Bills of Mortality and Halley's life table**: death counted, tabulated, and priced into annuities — statistics and the actuarial profession begin (Thread P).
- **1705/1758 — Halley's comet**: predicted by computation to return; it does, on schedule, sixteen years after Halley's death — hand calculation forecasting the heavens (Thread P).
- **1739 — Vaucanson's automata**: the flute player and the "digesting duck" make mechanical life a public sensation; his automated loom work leads directly to Jacquard.
- **1763 — Bayes' theorem** (published posthumously): belief updated by arithmetic — the mathematics now beating inside spam filters and AI.
- **1770 — The Mechanical Turk**: Kempelen's chess "automaton" conceals a human player and fools Europe for 84 years — the permanent lesson that claimed machine intelligence may hide human labor (Thread Q; Amazon later names its crowdwork platform after it).
- **1786 — William Playfair invents the line and bar chart** (pie chart 1801): data becomes picture (Thread P).
- **1790s — The metric system (and the failed decimal clock)**: revolutionary France rationalizes measurement; decimal time dies within two years — Sumer's base-60 hours outlast the guillotine (Thread F callback).
- **1700s — Human computing as a profession**: observatories and navies employ rooms of people grinding out tables (Nautical Almanac, 1767).
- **1790s — de Prony's logarithm project** (France): tables produced by a *pyramid of human computers* using division of labor — an assembly line for arithmetic, explicitly inspired by Adam Smith. A "computer" is a job title, not a machine.
- **1794 — The Chappe optical telegraph** (France): semaphore towers relay coded messages Paris–Lille in minutes, growing into a ~5,000 km state network with protocols, acknowledgments, error control, and encrypted payloads its own operators couldn't read; in 1834 the Blanc brothers bribe operators to smuggle bond-market signals through it — history's first network hack (Thread M).

## Era 5 — The Industrialization of Calculation (1800 – 1900)

- **c. 1801 — The blackboard** enters classrooms (popularized at West Point): the dominant educational display technology for the next two centuries.
- **1804 — Jacquard loom**: punched cards control weaving — programs stored on cards, data controlling machines.
- **1810s–40s — The Prussian school model**: age-graded classrooms, state curricula, and "normal schools" that turn teacher training into an institution — the template exported worldwide, and the machinery through which curriculum lag propagates (see Thread L).
- **1820–1851 — Thomas de Colmar's Arithmometer**: first commercially successful calculator; offices begin to mechanize.
- **1822 — Fourier's *Théorie analytique de la chaleur***: every signal is a sum of pure sine waves — the theorem that finally answers Mersenne's harmonic puzzle and later becomes, via the FFT, MP3s, Auto-Tune, and speech recognition (Thread T; Thread M).
- **1822 — Charles Babbage: Difference Engine**: automatic polynomial table calculation, motivated by errors in human-computed tables.
- **1827 — Solresol**: Sudre's constructed language built entirely from the seven solfège notes — speakable, singable, playable on a violin, or whistled across a valley (Thread T; Thread M).
- **1834 — The tally sticks burn Parliament**: centuries of split-tally records — matching halves as unforgeable medieval receipts — are carelessly incinerated, and the overloaded furnaces burn down the Houses of Parliament. Legacy-data destruction, spectacularly literal.
- **1837–44 — The electric telegraph** (Cooke & Wheatstone; Morse): messages become electric pulses; Morse code is a variable-length compression code — common letters short, rare letters long — a century before information theory named the idea.
- **1843 — Alexander Bain patents the fax**: images by wire decades *before* the telephone; Caselli's pantelegraph carries commercial fax traffic in 1860s France.
- **1837 — Babbage: Analytical Engine**: mill (CPU), store (memory), punched-card input, conditional branching — the general-purpose computer, designed a century early, never built.
- **1843 — Ada Lovelace's Notes**: first published algorithm intended for a machine (Bernoulli numbers); her insight that such a machine could manipulate *symbols*, not just numbers — even compose music.
- **1847/1854 — George Boole**: logic reduced to algebra of 0 and 1 — the mathematics that circuits would later speak.
- **1853 — Scheutz difference engine**: Babbage's idea actually built and sold.
- **1873–1887 — Office machine boom**: Odhner wheel, comptometer, cash register — arithmetic becomes clerical infrastructure.
- **1858/1866 — The transatlantic cable**: continents wired together; Kelvin's mirror galvanometer — sensitive enough to read a whisper of current after 3,000 km — makes it work, and per-word pricing spawns commercial codebooks: compression driven by economics.
- **1870 — Baudot's 5-bit code**: fixed-length binary character encoding for multiplexed telegraphy — direct ancestor of teleprinters, telex, and ASCII ("baud" preserves his name).
- **1863 — Helmholtz's *On the Sensations of Tone***: hearing itself dissected with tuned resonators — physiological acoustics founded (Thread T).
- **1857/1877 — Sound becomes data**: Scott de Martinville's phonautograph *records* sound it cannot play back; Edison's phonograph plays it — audio as stored signal (Thread M; Thread T).
- **1876/1891 — Telephone and automatic exchange**: Bell's telephone; then Strowger — an undertaker convinced the operator was diverting his business calls — invents dial switching: machine routing begins replacing human routing.
- **1880 — The photophone** (Bell & Tainter): voice carried 213 meters between Washington rooftops on a modulated beam of sunlight — no wires at all. Bell called it his greatest invention, greater than the telephone; the world had no use for it until fiber optics and free-space laser links vindicated the idea a century later (Thread M3).
- **1878–1950s — Switchboard operators**: overwhelmingly women, hand-routing the world's calls — the communication twin of the human computers (Thread B).
- **1801 — Gauss finds Ceres**: least squares recovers a lost dwarf planet from 41 days of observations — the mathematics of taming measurement error, computed by hand (Thread P).
- **1811–16 — The Luddites**: skilled weavers smash stocking frames — not hatred of technology but a labor revolt against immiseration; the corrective card for history's most misused insult (Thread Q).
- **1846 — Neptune discovered "with the point of his pen"**: Le Verrier computes an unseen planet's position from Uranus' wobbles; astronomers find it within one degree — hand computation's greatest triumph.
- **1858 — Nightingale's rose diagrams**: statistical graphics as political weapon — army sanitation reformed by chart (Thread P).
- **1869 — Jevons' "logic piano"**: a machine that performs Boolean inference — mechanical logic, halfway between Boole and Shannon.
- **1880s–1901 — Galton and Pearson**: correlation and regression, the modern statistical toolkit — created in deep entanglement with eugenics: a card teaching that computation is not morally neutral (Thread P).
- **1890 — Herman Hollerith**: punched-card tabulators process the US Census in record time; his company later merges into **IBM**.
- **1891 — Genaille–Lucas rulers**: Napier's bones perfected — multiplication by pure sight-reading, no carrying; a reminder that "calculating aids" kept evolving right up to the electronic age.
- **1893 — The Committee of Ten** (US): secondary mathematics standardized into arithmetic → algebra → geometry → trigonometry — the course sequence most students still walk today, frozen before the airplane flew (see Thread L).

## Era 6 — Human Computers, Analog Machines & Theory (1900 – 1940)

- **1870s–1920s — Harvard Observatory computers** (Williamina Fleming, Annie Jump Cannon, Henrietta Leavitt): women "computers" classify hundreds of thousands of stars; Leavitt's computed relation lets us measure the universe.
- **1901 — Marconi's transatlantic wireless**: the letter "S" crosses an ocean without wires; radio makes signals *broadcast* rather than routed.
- **1908 — Felix Klein names the "double discontinuity"**: university mathematics and school mathematics fail to connect in either direction — the feedback loop that lets curricula lag the frontier by generations (Thread L); Klein founds the first international commission on mathematics teaching the same year.
- **1900/1928 — Hilbert's program**: 23 problems, then the Entscheidungsproblem — can all mathematics be mechanically decided? "We must know, we will know": the dare that Gödel and Turing answer.
- **1910 — Otlet's Mundaneum** (Brussels): 12+ million index cards answering queries by mail and telegraph — a paper search engine; by 1934 Otlet sketches networked screens querying it remotely (Thread S).
- **1912–20 — Torres Quevedo**: a *working* chess endgame automaton (no hidden human this time), essays anticipating floating-point arithmetic, and a remotely commanded electromechanical calculator — Spain's forgotten bridge between Babbage and the electronic age.
- **1922 — Richardson's "forecast factory"**: a fantasy of 64,000 human computers in a vast hall predicting weather in real time — the dream ENIAC fulfills in 1950.
- **1920s — R.A. Fisher**: design of experiments, significance testing, modern statistics — forged at an agricultural research station and now the operating system of empirical science (Thread P).
- **1931 — Gödel's incompleteness theorems**: any sufficiently rich formal system contains truths it cannot prove — mechanized reason has hard limits, discovered before the machines even arrive.
- **1872–20th c. — Tide-predicting machines** (Kelvin and successors): analog gear-and-pulley computers used into the 1960s (including for D-Day planning).
- **1912–1945 — Naval fire-control computers** (Dreyer Table, Ford Rangekeeper, the US Navy's Mark 1): shipboard mechanical analog computers solving moving-target ballistics in real time; analog gunnery computers served at sea into the 1990s.
- **1936 — Vladimir Lukyanov's water integrator** (USSR): partial differential equations solved by water levels in interconnected glass tubes — originally for heat flow in concrete; mass-produced and used in Soviet engineering for decades (see Thread I).
- **1927–1931 — Vannevar Bush's differential analyzer**: room-sized analog computer solving differential equations.
- **1935–1938 — WPA Mathematical Tables Project**: hundreds of human computers employed during the Depression producing math tables.
- **1920s–1940 — William & Elizebeth Friedman**: the married founders of modern American cryptanalysis — Elizebeth breaks rum-runner and Nazi spy networks (credit classified for decades; the FBI took it); William's team breaks Japan's PURPLE machine (1940) without ever seeing one — reconstructing a computer from its outputs alone.
- **1936 — Alan Turing, "On Computable Numbers"**: the universal machine; a precise definition of "computation" itself — and of its limits.
- **1936 — Alonzo Church: lambda calculus** (with the Church–Turing thesis emerging).
- **1937 — Claude Shannon's master's thesis**: Boolean algebra = switching circuits. Logic becomes hardware.
- **1938–1941 — Konrad Zuse: Z1–Z3**: the Z3 (1941) is the first working programmable, fully automatic digital computer (binary, electromechanical).

## Era 7 — The Electronic Breakthrough (1940 – 1955)

- **1942 — Atanasoff–Berry Computer**: first electronic digital calculating device (not programmable).
- **1943–44 — Colossus** (Bletchley Park): first programmable electronic computer, breaking German ciphers; after the war most are dismantled and the drawings burned by order — secret for 30 years, full details classified until 2000.
- **1943 — SIGSALY**: the first secure digital voice system (and first PCM transmission) — 50-ton terminals carrying Churchill–Roosevelt calls; its existence stayed secret until 1976.
- **1943–45 — Turing's Delilah**: a portable encrypted-voice machine Turing built in near-total secrecy; the technical report wasn't released for nearly 70 years.
- **1944 — D-Day's tides**: Arthur Doodson computes invasion tides on Kelvin-type tide-predicting machines from anonymized harmonic constants for a location he knows only as "Position Z" — the computer wasn't cleared to know it was Normandy.
- **1944 — Harvard Mark I** (Aiken/IBM): electromechanical giant; Grace Hopper programs it; the famous "bug" (an actual moth) comes later on Mark II.
- **1945 — ENIAC**: ~5,000 additions per second — a thousand times faster than any person. Programmed by six women (Jean Bartik, Betty Holberton, et al.) whose work went unrecognized for decades.
- **1945–46 — ENIAC's first real job (classified)**: hydrogen-bomb feasibility calculations on a million punched cards; at Los Alamos, von Neumann and Ulam's **Monte Carlo method** is born classified, following wartime rooms of human computers and Feynman's punched-card "assembly line."
- **1945 — von Neumann's EDVAC report**: the stored-program architecture nearly every computer still uses.
- **1946–49 — Manchester Baby (1948) & EDSAC (1949)**: first stored-program computers actually running.
- **1949 — The MONIAC** (Bill Phillips, LSE): the national economy simulated hydraulically — income, taxes, savings, and imports as colored water flowing through tanks and valves; about 14 were built for universities and central banks.
- **1947 — The transistor** (Bardeen, Brattain, Shockley at Bell Labs).
- **1948 — Shannon's "A Mathematical Theory of Communication"**: the bit; information becomes a quantity.
- **1948 — The Curta**: a pocket-sized mechanical calculator of astonishing precision, designed by Curt Herzstark while imprisoned in Buchenwald; beloved by engineers and rally drivers until the 1970s.
- **1950 — Hamming codes**: error-*correcting* codes, born of weekend frustration with crashing punched-card runs — mathematics that lets messages heal themselves; Reed–Solomon codes (1960) later shield everything from CDs to deep-space probes.
- **1943 — McCulloch & Pitts**: neurons modeled as logic gates — the paper von Neumann cites in the EDVAC report; brains and computers become each other's mirrors.
- **1945 — Vannevar Bush's Memex** ("As We May Think"): an imagined desk that links documents by association — the essay that inspires Engelbart, Nelson, and ultimately HyperCard and the Web (Thread S).
- **1948 — Wiener's *Cybernetics***: feedback and control unify machines, animals, and organizations — a surprise bestseller that makes "thinking machines" a public question.
- **1950 — ENIAC computes the first numerical weather forecasts**: Richardson's 1922 fantasy, realized in 28 years.
- **1951 — LEO I**: the world's first business computer — built by J. Lyons & Co., a British *teashop chain*, to schedule cakes and payroll (Thread R).
- **1950 — Turing's "Computing Machinery and Intelligence"**: can machines think? The imitation game.
- **1951 — UNIVAC I**: computers go commercial; predicts the 1952 election on live TV.
- **1952 — Grace Hopper's A-0 compiler**: programs that write programs; path to human-readable code.

## Era 8 — Computation Becomes Infrastructure (1955 – 1975)

- **1957 — FORTRAN**: scientists stop writing machine code; formulas become programs.
- **1957 — Computer music begins**: the *Illiac Suite* (Hiller & Isaacson), the first substantial computer-composed score, and Max Mathews' MUSIC I at Bell Labs, the first widely used sound-synthesis program — Kircher's 1650 composition box, electrified (Thread T).
- **1955–58 — Calculus reaches high school; the New Math follows**: AP Calculus debuts 268 years after Newton's *Principia*; then Sputnik panic pushes set theory into elementary classrooms via teachers who were never trained in it — the curriculum-lag engine demonstrated at national scale, and reversed within 15 years (Thread L).
- **1958 — Setun** (Moscow State University): the only production *balanced-ternary* computer — digits −1, 0, +1; about 50 built; a road not taken.
- **1953–58 — Core memory and SAGE**: Forrester's magnetic cores (every bit threaded by hand, mostly by women textile workers) power SAGE air defense — 24 networked vacuum-tube giants, the largest computers ever built, birthplace of the modem (Threads O, J).
- **1954–66 — Machine translation: hype and crash**: the Georgetown demo promises translation "solved within five years"; the 1966 ALPAC report kills funding — AI's first boom-and-winter cycle (Thread N).
- **1956 — The Dartmouth workshop**: "artificial intelligence" is named, with a proposal expecting major progress from ten men in two months.
- **1956 — IBM RAMAC 305**: the first hard drive — 5 MB in a one-ton cabinet, leased at $3,200/month (Thread O).
- **1958 — Lisp (McCarthy) and the perceptron (Rosenblatt)**: the two souls of AI — symbols and neurons; the Navy demos a learning machine to breathless headlines, and Minsky & Papert's *Perceptrons* (1969) freezes neural nets for a generation.
- **1960 — PLATO**: computerized education with touch screens, message boards, and multiplayer games — online community, decades early (Threads L, S).
- **1961 — Landauer's principle**: erasing one bit has a minimum energy cost — computation *is* physics; the ultimate floor beneath Thread A's energy figures.
- **1962 — Spacewar!** (MIT PDP-1): the video game — play will eventually bankroll the GPU industry that powers AI.
- **1963 — Lorenz discovers chaos**: re-entering rounded numbers into a weather model reveals sensitive dependence — some computations have *mathematical* forecast horizons no hardware can beat (Thread N).
- **1965 — The FFT (Cooley–Tukey) and "hypertext" (Nelson)**: the algorithm that makes signal processing feasible (with roots in Gauss's notebooks), and the word that names linked text (Thread S) — one year, two futures.
- **1966 — ELIZA**: Weizenbaum's 200-line chatbot "therapist"; users pour out their hearts to pattern-matching, and Weizenbaum spends the rest of his life warning about what he'd seen.
- **1966–69 — The Apollo Guidance Computer**: Margaret Hamilton's team writes the software that lands on the Moon (she popularizes the term "software engineering"); its core-*rope* program memory is hand-woven, wire by wire, by women factory workers — "LOL memory." Weaving literally becomes software: Jacquard's loop, closed (Threads B, O, Q).
- **1962 — NSA's Harvest**: a one-of-a-kind IBM supercomputer built solely for codebreaking; the agency quietly becomes the world's largest employer of mathematicians and a hidden patron of the computer industry.
- **1970–74 — Public-key cryptography invented in secret**: Ellis, Cocks, and Williamson at GCHQ discover "non-secret encryption" — the RSA and Diffie–Hellman ideas — years before the public did; classified until 1997 (see Thread J).
- **1974 — The Ultra secret breaks**: Winterbotham's *The Ultra Secret* reveals Bletchley Park; thousands of oath-bound veterans finally learn what the person at the next desk had been doing — and the history of computing has to be rewritten.
- **1958–59 — Integrated circuit** (Kilby, Noyce).
- **1950s–60s — The golden age of electronic analog computers**: patch-panel machines built from op-amps ("operational" = performing mathematical operations) solve differential equations for aerospace and industry — often faster than early digital machines; fluidic logic (gates made of nothing but jets and channels) computes with air and water, no electronics at all; hybrid analog–digital installations linger into the 1980s.
- **1950s–60s — NASA's human computers** (Katherine Johnson, Dorothy Vaughan, Mary Jackson): trajectories computed by hand; John Glenn asks Johnson to *check the electronic computer's numbers by hand* before flying — the handoff moment between human and machine computation.
- **1964 — IBM System/360**: the mainframe standard; BASIC brings programming to students.
- **1965 — Moore's Law** stated: transistor counts double roughly every two years.
- **1968 — Engelbart's "Mother of All Demos"**: mouse, windows, hypertext, video calls — in one demo.
- **1968–71 — Computer algebra begins (Macsyma)**: machines now do *symbolic* mathematics — algebra and calculus, not just arithmetic.
- **1969 — ARPANET**: computers begin talking to each other — built on packet switching (Baran, Davies): messages chopped into independently routed fragments; email (1971) becomes the surprise killer app; TCP/IP (designed 1974, adopted 1983) becomes the internet's common tongue.
- **1971 — Intel 4004**: the microprocessor — a computer on a chip.
- **1972 — HP-35 pocket scientific calculator**: the slide rule dies within a decade. C and Unix are born the same era.
- **1976 — Appel & Haken prove the Four Color Theorem by computer**: the first major theorem whose proof *requires* machine computation — mathematics itself changed.

## Era 9 — Personal, Symbolic & Networked (1975 – 2005)

- **1968–73 — Dynabook and Xerox PARC's Alto**: Kay imagines a computer-book for children; PARC builds the GUI, ethernet, and laser printing — the future assembled a decade early, famously toured by Steve Jobs (Thread S).
- **1975–77 — Altair 8800, Apple II, Commodore PET, TRS-80**: computers enter homes.
- **1976–77 — Public-key cryptography (Diffie–Hellman, RSA)**: security built on computational *hardness* — the difficulty of factoring becomes a lock protecting the world's communications.
- **1979 — VisiCalc**: the spreadsheet — arithmetic automated for everyone with a desk; the "killer app."
- **1980 — Seymour Papert's *Mindstorms* and Logo**: children programming turtles as a way of *doing* mathematics — constructionism's high-water mark, largely washed back out of schools within two decades.
- **1981 — Feynman, "Simulating Physics with Computers"**: nature is quantum-mechanical; proposes quantum computers.
- **1987 — HyperCard** (Bill Atkinson, Apple): linked cards, home-made software, hypertext for the masses — the design inspiration for this very site.
- **1970s–80s — Expert systems and the AI winters**: MYCIN and XCON boom; the Lighthill report (1973) and the late-80s collapse bust — AI's hype cycle runs twice before 2012, a pattern every modern student should know (Thread N).
- **1976 — Cray-1**: supercomputing's icon — 160 megaflops in a C-shaped couch with its own upholstered seating.
- **1983 — MIDI**: musical performance becomes a real-time data protocol — every synthesizer on Earth learns one language (Thread T).
- **1984 — Macintosh**: the GUI goes mass-market; HyperCard follows three years later (Thread S).
- **1985 — IEEE 754 floating point** (Kahan): the number format standardized — the *actual arithmetic* of virtually every FLOP in Thread A's ladder; also the format whose subtleties cause several of Thread N's disasters.
- **1986 — Backpropagation popularized** (Rumelhart, Hinton, Williams): multi-layer neural networks learn internal representations — the seed that flowers in 2012.
- **1988 — Mathematica** (and Maple, 1982): full computer algebra systems; a laptop can now outperform any historical mathematician at symbolic manipulation.
- **1990 — TI-81 graphing calculator**: machine computation lands on every student's school desk — the classroom debate over "calculators vs. by-hand" begins in earnest.
- **1973–99 — Ethnomathematics becomes a field**: Zaslavsky's *Africa Counts* (1973), D'Ambrosio naming the discipline (1984), the Aschers' mathematical decoding of quipus, Gerdes on sona geometry, Eglash's *African Fractals* (1999) — scholarship finally documents the world's mathematical traditions on their own terms (see Thread K).
- **1994 — Kaktovik numerals**: Iñupiaq middle-school students in Alaska *invent* a base-20 numeral system to fit their language's counting words; it spreads across Inuit communities and enters Unicode in 2022 — proof that numeral systems are still being born.
- **1989–2000s — NCTM standards and the "Math Wars"**: reform vs. back-to-basics battles over calculators, standard algorithms, and understanding — every side arguing, mostly without the history, about a 4,000-year-old question (Thread L).
- **1991 — World Wide Web** goes public (Berners-Lee, 1989 proposal).
- **1990s — The Crypto Wars**: strong encryption legally classed as a *munition*; PGP's Phil Zimmermann under criminal investigation for "exporting" code; RSA printed on T-shirts as civil disobedience; the Clipper chip's key-escrow scheme defeated — mathematics itself contested as a weapon.
- **1994 — Shor's algorithm**: a quantum computer could factor huge numbers efficiently — threatening RSA and igniting the race to build one.
- **1994 — Leonard Adleman's DNA computer**: a seven-city Hamiltonian-path problem solved by molecules in a test tube — biochemistry as processor.
- **1996 — GIMPS**: the Great Internet Mersenne Prime Search — volunteers' idle PCs hunt primes of the form 2^p−1, and nearly every record prime since is one; SETI@home (1999) takes distributed volunteer computing mainstream. The friar of *Harmonie universelle* is still on computation's leaderboard (Thread T).
- **1997 — Deep Blue defeats Kasparov**: brute-force search beats the world chess champion (~200 million positions/second).
- **1998 — Google**: computation organizes the world's information.
- **1985–2000 — The famous failures**: Therac-25's radiation deaths, the Patriot missile's clock drift (1991), the Pentium FDIV bug (1994), Ariane 5's overflow explosion (1996), the Mars Climate Orbiter's unit mix-up (1999), and Y2K (2000) — machines compute wrong exactly as fast as they compute right (Thread N).
- **1999 — GPU** (term coined with NVIDIA GeForce 256): graphics chips that will later, unexpectedly, power AI.

## Era 10 — The Age of Massive Computation & AI (2005 – present)

- **2005 — Printer tracking dots decoded**: researchers reveal that color laser printers secretly stamp nearly every page with faint yellow dots encoding the printer's serial number and a timestamp — steganography hiding in plain sight in every office.
- **2006–07 — Cloud computing (AWS) and CUDA**: renting oceans of compute; GPUs opened to general math.
- **2007 — The iPhone**: every channel in Thread M and a supercomputer, fused into a pocket slab — within 15 years, most of humanity computes constantly without ever calling it computing.
- **2010 — Stuxnet**: malware physically destroys uranium centrifuges — computation as kinetic weapon (Thread J).
- **2011 — IBM Watson wins Jeopardy!**: language question-answering at champion level — the decade's opening bell.
- **2013 — The Snowden disclosures**: the public learns the scale of computational mass surveillance (Thread J).
- **2008 — Petascale (Roadrunner)**: 10¹⁵ operations per second.
- **2010–14 — Common Core & the CS-for-all push**: parents nationwide discover they can't help with elementary homework — the generational gap made visible at the kitchen table; code.org (2013) and England's national computing curriculum (2014) attempt to close an 80-year CS teaching gap; Conrad Wolfram campaigns for computer-based math education (Thread L).
- **2012 — AlexNet / ImageNet moment**: deep learning works, powered by GPUs; modern AI era begins.
- **2016 — AlphaGo defeats Lee Sedol**: intuition-like play from neural networks plus search.
- **2017 — "Attention Is All You Need"**: the transformer architecture behind modern language models.
- **2020–2022 — GPT-3, then ChatGPT**: large language models reach the public; training runs consume ~10²³–10²⁵ floating-point operations.
- **2022 — Frontier supercomputer**: first official exascale machine — over 10¹⁸ operations per second.
- **2000s–2010s — Nature computes**: slime mold (Physarum) re-derives the Tokyo rail network layout (2010); researchers use ripples in a literal bucket of water as a "liquid state machine" for speech-pattern recognition (2003) — see Thread I.
- **2019 — Google's Sycamore "quantum supremacy" claim**: a quantum processor performs a (contrived) task believed intractable for classical machines.
- **2020 — AlphaFold 2**: protein structure prediction effectively solved — computation as an instrument of scientific *discovery*, honored with a Nobel Prize in 2024.
- **2020 — Operation Rubicon revealed**: Crypto AG, the trusted Swiss cipher-machine maker, had been secretly owned by the CIA and West German BND for decades — half the world bought its "secure" machines from its adversaries.
- **2024 — NIST post-quantum cryptography standards**: computation's future begins reshaping its own security.
- **Today — computing still classified**: intelligence-agency supercomputers absent from public rankings; TEMPEST emission-security standards partly secret; nuclear-stockpile machines behind closed doors — the fastest computers on Earth may not be the ones we know about (see Thread J).
- **2020s — Frontier AI training runs (~10²⁵–10²⁶ FLOPs)** and formal proof assistants (Lean, Coq) verifying real mathematics — machines now assist at every level from arithmetic to proof.
- **2020s — The analog revival**: in-memory, memristor, and photonic chips perform AI's matrix arithmetic with raw physics (currents, light) instead of digital logic — computing's oldest idea returning at the frontier, driven by AI's appetite for multiplication (Thread A).

### Thread A — "The Speed Ladder" (the emotional core of the site)
Approximate sustained arithmetic rates, for direct comparison cards:
- Human with pencil: ~0.01–0.05 operations/sec (a multi-digit multiplication takes ~20–60 s)

- de Prony's table project: ~100 human computers for years → millions of table entries
- Mechanical desk calculator: ~0.1–0.5 ops/sec
- ENIAC (1945): ~5×10³ additions/sec
- IBM System/360 (1964): ~10⁵–10⁶ ops/sec
- Cray-1 (1976): ~1.6×10⁸ FLOP/s
- Intel Pentium (1993): ~10⁸
- Smartphone (2020s): ~10¹¹–10¹²
- Single modern AI GPU: ~10¹⁴–10¹⁵ FLOP/s
- Frontier/exascale cluster: >10¹⁸ FLOP/s
- One frontier AI training run: ~10²⁵–10²⁶ total FLOPs
- **The punchline card**: a student doing one operation per second, nonstop, would need vastly longer than the age of the universe (~4×10¹⁷ seconds) to perform the arithmetic of a single modern AI training run — yet every one of those operations is the same kind of arithmetic the student practices by hand.

### Thread F — "What Is a Number?" (cross-cultural conceptions of number & data)

**Featured comparison stack: Egypt vs. Greece**
- *Egypt — number as measure.* Number lives in physical quantities: the cubit (length), setat (area), hekat (grain volume), deben (weight), the 365-day civil calendar (time). Unit fractions exist for fair division — how to split 7 loaves among 10 workers. Mathematics is a scribe's craft, taught by worked example, validated by *working*: "the correct method of reckoning."
- *Greece — number as idea.* Sharp split between *arithmos* (a multitude of discrete units) and *megethos* (continuous magnitude), and between *logistikē* (mere reckoning — low prestige, left to slaves and merchants) and *arithmētikē* (theory of number — philosophy). Mathematics is validated by *proof*: it must be shown to be necessarily true.
- *The twist:* Herodotus reports the Greeks learned geometry from Egyptian rope-stretchers. Measurement became abstraction; the surveyor's craft became the philosopher's object.
- *Contrast cards:* procedure vs. proof · scribe vs. philosopher · "does it work?" vs. "why must it be true?" · number embedded in things vs. number as a thing itself.
- *Payoff card for students:* every math student recapitulates both traditions — first you *compute* like an Egyptian scribe, then you learn to *prove* like a Greek. Both are the tradition.

**Other conception-of-number cards:**
- *Mesopotamia:* no single "number" at first — separate metrological systems for grain, land, silver, and time; the abstract sexagesimal system emerges from bureaucracy. Our hours, minutes, and degrees are its living fossil.
- *China:* number as a physical rod on a counting board — manipulable, spatial; negative numbers simply rods of another color.
- *India:* numbers as Sanskrit words and verse (mathematics recited and memorized); zero and the infinite admitted as legitimate objects of calculation.
- *Maya:* number as time — dates, calendar cycles, numbers personified as gods; base-20 with head-glyph numerals.
- *Inca:* data without writing — knot type, knot position, cord color, and cord hierarchy as the fields of an imperial database.
- *Bodies and bases:* base-5, 10, 20, 60; 27-body-part counting systems of Papua New Guinea; Yoruba base-20 with subtractive construction — the human body as the first numeral system.
- *Data as length, weight, time, quantity:* a synthesis card — before number was abstract, every culture computed *with the world itself*: ropes, stones, beads, knots, shadows, water clocks, balance scales.

**Computing without written numbers (embodied & ritual computation):**
- *I Ching* (China): 64 hexagrams from binary lines — divination as combinatorics (see Era 2 and the Leibniz connection).
- *Ifá divination* (Yoruba, West Africa): 256 odu generated from 8 binary casts — exactly one byte of states, each indexing a body of recited literature; a binary addressing system in ritual form.
- *Sona sand drawings* (Chokwe, Angola/DR Congo): single-line figures traced over dot grids by memorized rules — Eulerian paths and recursive algorithms executed as art.
- *Marshall Islands stick charts*: ocean swell patterns and their interference around islands encoded in lashed sticks and shells — an analog model of wave dynamics, memorized before the voyage and left ashore.
- *Polynesian wayfinding*: star compass, dead reckoning, and the etak reference-island system — voyages of thousands of miles computed continuously in the navigator's head.
- *Mancala-family games* (Africa, Middle East, Asia): seed-counting and capture strategy — arithmetic and lookahead as play.
- Theme card: computation has always lived in ritual, art, navigation, and games — alongside and long before written mathematics.

### Thread G — Cryptography: computing to conceal and reveal
Caesar cipher (antiquity) → al-Kindi's frequency analysis (9th c.) → Alberti's cipher disk (1467) → Vigenère (1553) → Babbage & Kasiski break it (1850s–63) → Rejewski's bomba vs. Enigma (1932) → Turing/Welchman Bombe & Colossus (1940s) → DES (1977) → Diffie–Hellman & RSA (1976–77) → elliptic curves → Shor's quantum threat (1994) → NIST post-quantum standards (2024). ### Thread H — The Art of Reckoning (how people *actually* calculated)

**H1. The Roman paradox: numerals for writing, machines for computing**
- Roman numerals are a *recording* notation, nearly useless for arithmetic — try multiplying XLVII × XXXIX on paper. Romans didn't; nobody did.
- Day-to-day arithmetic ran on **pebbles (calculi) on lined counting boards** and on the **portable bronze hand abacus** — grooved slots for units through millions, plus extra slots for base-12 fractions (unciae). A merchant's pocket device, ~2,000 years before the pocket calculator.
- Key card for students: the numeral was the *output format*; the board was the *processor*. Notation ≠ computation.
- Linguistic-fossils card: *calculus/calculate* (pebble), *exchequer* (the checkered counting cloth of the English treasury), the shop *counter* (the counting table), *bank* (the money-changer's bench where the board sat).
- Counting boards with cast counters (jetons) remained standard across Europe into the 1600s–1700s. Samuel Pepys — Cambridge graduate, senior naval administrator — hired a tutor at age 29 (1662) to learn the multiplication table. Written arithmetic was a *specialist skill* long after board-reckoning was universal.

**H2. The living abacus: East Asian abacus education today**
- The **suanpan** (China, 2+5 beads) and **soroban** (Japan, 1+4 beads) are not museum pieces — millions of children in Japan, China, Taiwan, Korea, India, and Southeast Asia train on them today in schools and after-school academies, with **kyū/dan ranking exams** graded like martial arts.
- The **schoty**, Russia's abacus, was still clicking on shop counters into the 1990s. Western Europe abandoned the counting board; most of the world never stopped.
- **Anzan (暗算) — the internalized machine**: advanced students stop needing the physical soroban. They visualize it and move imaginary beads — you can watch champions' fingers flicking over an empty desk. The machine has been *installed in the mind*.
- **Flash anzan**: multi-digit numbers flashed on screen faster than they can be read aloud; top competitors correctly sum 15 three-digit numbers shown in under two seconds. National soroban championships and the Mental Calculation World Cup keep the tradition elite and alive.
- Research on expert abacus users suggests they shift arithmetic from verbal processing toward **visuospatial imagery** — they compute with a mental picture rather than with inner speech.
- **The payoff card (core to the site's thesis):** the tool does not replace the skill — practiced deeply enough, the tool *becomes* the skill. A student who masters a calculating machine ends up carrying it in their head. This is the strongest answer to "why practice by hand when machines exist?"

**H3. Before Napier: the toolkit of tricks**
- **Finger reckoning (dactylonomy)**: Bede's system to 9,999; Mediterranean and Middle Eastern market-traders' finger systems; complement-based finger multiplication for the 6–10 times tables — arithmetic carried on the body.
- **Doubling and halving**: Egyptian multiplication by repeated doubling, and its folk survival, "Russian peasant multiplication" — secretly *binary multiplication*, the method inside every CPU today. (Superb card: the pharaoh's scribe and the microchip run the same algorithm.)
- **Table lookup as a way of life**: Babylonian multiplication and reciprocal tablets; Ptolemy's chords; medieval computus tables for Easter; Toledan and Alfonsine astronomical tables. For most of history, "computing" largely meant *looking up* someone else's frozen labor.
- **Quarter-square multiplication**: ab = [(a+b)² − (a−b)²]/4 — multiply using only a table of squares plus addition and subtraction; used from Babylon to 19th-century table books.
- **Lattice (gelosia) multiplication**: the grid method from Indian/Islamic mathematics, standard in the abbaco schools — and now back in modern elementary classrooms.
- **Casting out nines**: the universal error-check of hand arithmetic, transmitted through al-Khwarizmi's tradition — checksum logic centuries before checksums.
- **Prosthaphaeresis**: trigonometric identities as a multiplication machine (see Era 4) — proof that the *hunger* for fast multiplication predates the means.

**H4. After Napier: three centuries of augmented hand-calculation**
- **Logarithm tables** (Briggs onward): the single most important calculating aid of the scientific era; engineers, navigators, gunners, and schoolchildren computed with them into the 1970s.
- **The slide rule**: logarithms made physical — analog multiplication at a glance; "slipstick" culture, belt holsters, carried aboard Apollo as backup.
- **Ready reckoners**: cheap printed books of precomputed prices, interest, wages, and conversions — the shopkeeper's lookup table from the 1600s well into the 20th century.
- **Nomograms** (d'Ocagne, 1880s): computation as *drawing* — lay a straightedge across printed scales and read off the answer; used for artillery, engineering, and medicine (some clinical nomograms survive today).
- **Planimeters and integraphs**: trace a curve's outline with a stylus, read its area off a dial — mechanical integration.
- **Specialized aids to the very end**: Genaille–Lucas rulers (1891) and dozens of similar devices sold to clerks and accountants into the 20th century.

**H5. Lightning minds: mental calculators and speed systems**
- **Professional lightning calculators**: Zerah Colburn (factoring large numbers mentally as a child, 1810s), George Parker Bidder (from stage calculator to great railway engineer), Alexander Aitken (perhaps the best-documented calculating mind), Shakuntala Devi (multiplied two 13-digit numbers in 28 seconds, 1980).
- **The Trachtenberg system**: high-speed arithmetic rules developed by Jakow Trachtenberg *entirely in his head while imprisoned in Nazi camps* — calculation as survival of the mind.
- **"Vedic mathematics"** (Bharati Krishna Tirtha, pub. 1965): sutra-based speed techniques ("all from 9 and the last from 10," "vertically and crosswise"); a modern system rather than an ancient Vedic one, but pedagogically potent and hugely popular in India.
- **Everyday mental tricks** as mini-cards: complements ("make ten"), left-to-right addition, ×5 via ×10÷2, ×25 via ×100÷4, squaring numbers ending in 5, and 47×99 = 4,700 − 47.
- **Closing card**: when calculation was economically vital, societies invested enormously in *human* computational skill — tables, tools, tricks, and training. Today that need is met by machines running 10¹⁸ operations per second — but the trained mind remains the only place where *understanding* happens.

### Thread I — Computing with the World: water, analog & unconventional machines

**I1. Water computing**
- **Clepsydrae**: from Egyptian outflow bowls to Ctesibius' feedback-regulated clocks (the first known feedback control) to elaborate Chinese and Islamic water-clock towers (Su Song 1088, al-Jazari 1206) — time itself was water-computed for three millennia.
- **Lukyanov's water integrator (1936)**: PDEs as plumbing — water levels are the variables, tube resistances the coefficients; adjust the valves, read the solution. Mass-produced in the USSR, used for dams, mines, and rocketry into the 1980s.
- **The MONIAC (1949)**: Keynesian macroeconomics in transparent tanks — raise taxes and literally watch consumption drain. Fourteen built; one still runs demonstrations in New Zealand's central bank museum.
- **Fluidics (1960s)**: logic gates made of nothing but jets and channels — no electronics, immune to radiation and EMP; used in aircraft, industrial control, even missile systems; reborn today as **microfluidic logic** in lab-on-a-chip devices.
- **The liquid brain (2003)**: researchers used ripples on the surface of a bucket of water as the reservoir in a "liquid state machine," successfully distinguishing spoken words — a literal bucket of water performing pattern recognition.
- Theme card: water was humanity's first continuous quantity. Time, then economies, then differential equations were all made to *flow*.

**I2. Mechanical & electrical analog computing**
- **The integrator lineage**: James Thomson's ball-and-disk integrator → his brother Lord Kelvin's tide predictor and harmonic analyzer → Bush's differential analyzer (Era 6) — continuous mathematics done by wheels.
- **Fire control**: Dreyer Tables and Rangekeepers through the Mark 1 — shipboard analog computers tracking moving targets from moving platforms in real time, decades before digital could; still aboard recommissioned battleships in the 1990s.
- **Field analogies**: electrolytic tanks and conductive paper "compute" electric and aerodynamic fields; stretched rubber sheets solve potential problems — let the physics find the answer.
- **Network analyzers**: scale-model power grids used to compute the behavior of real power grids into the 1960s.
- **The op-amp era**: room-sized patch-panel analog computers simulate aircraft, rockets, and reactors; the very name "operational amplifier" means *an amplifier that performs mathematical operations*.
- Theme card: for continuous problems — tides, trajectories, flight — analog machines were *faster than digital for decades*. Digital won on precision and programmability, not on any monopoly over mathematics.

**I3. Graphical computing: the nomograph, expanded**
- **Graphical statics (1860s)**: bridges and roofs designed by drawing force polygons — Eiffel-era engineering computed with straightedge and pencil.
- **Nomography proper (d'Ocagne, 1884)**: alignment charts where laying a straightedge across three scales *is* the calculation; grounded in projective geometry and determinants.
- **War by straightedge**: WWI and WWII artillery fired on nomogram-computed solutions; engineering handbooks carried alignment charts for everything from pipe flow to beam loads.
- **Still alive in medicine**: clinical nomograms (cardiac risk, drug dosing, oncology prognosis) remain in use — a 19th-century computing technology at the bedside.
- **Pedagogy card**: students can *build* a working nomogram for the ideal gas law or Ohm's law with paper and ruler — constructing a computer that has no moving parts at all.

**I4. Nature computes: unconventional and physical computation**
- **Soap films**: dip a wire frame, and the film instantly settles into a minimal surface — near-instant "solutions" to problems (Plateau, Steiner trees) that strain digital computers.
- **Kitchen analog**: spaghetti sort (cut rods to length, tap the bundle — the tallest is found in one step); string boards that find shortest paths by pulling taut.
- **Toys that are computers**: dominoes as logic gates; marble machines (Digi-Comp II, today's Turing Tumble); the Tinkertoy computer (Hillis & Silverman, MIT) that plays perfect tic-tac-toe.
- **The billiard-ball computer** (Fredkin & Toffoli, 1982): a thought experiment showing computation from nothing but elastic collisions — foundational to reversible and quantum computing.
- **Wet computers**: Adleman's DNA computation (1994); molecular logic gates; slime mold (*Physarum*) solving mazes (2000) and re-deriving the Tokyo rail network (2010).
- **Cellular automata**: Conway's Game of Life (1970) — trivial rules yielding universal computation; Wolfram's systematic exploration.
- **Reservoir computing**: any sufficiently rich dynamical system — water, lasers, mechanical springs — can serve as a machine-learning substrate.
- **The analog AI revival (2020s)**: memristor crossbars multiply matrices via Ohm's and Kirchhoff's laws; photonic chips do it with interference of light — physics-as-processor returning precisely because AI's arithmetic appetite (Thread A) has outgrown comfortable digital scaling.
- **Closing card**: digital was a choice, not a destiny. Anything with reliable physics can compute. Electronics won on precision, programmability, and perfect copying — but the world itself has been doing mathematics all along, and students should know the machine in their pocket is one answer among many.

### Thread J — The Secret History: computation classified

**J1. Steganography: hiding the message itself**
- Ancient roots: Herodotus' tattooed scalp and wax-tablet warnings (Era 2); Aeneas Tacticus' catalogue of hidden-message tricks.
- Invisible inks from antiquity through both World Wars; the Cardan grille (1550) — a mask that turns an innocent letter into a cipher; null ciphers where only every nth word counts.
- **Microdots** (WWII): whole pages photographed down to the size of a period — J. Edgar Hoover called them "the enemy's masterpiece of espionage."
- **Printer tracking dots**: the machine identification code secretly stamped by color laser printers, publicly decoded in 2005 — a hidden message in *your* documents.
- Digital steganography and watermarking today — including proposals to statistically watermark AI-generated text: steganography contemplated at the scale of trillions of words.
- Card theme: cryptography scrambles a message; steganography denies it exists. Both are computation bent toward concealment.

**J2. The machines themselves were secret**
- **Colossus erased**: ten machines; most dismantled and the drawings burned by order after the war; engineer Tommy Flowers, forbidden to speak, watches ENIAC take the crown of "first" — full details classified until 2000. Textbooks taught a wrong history for half a century.
- **The Bombes**: British and US Navy models (600 WAVES ran NCR-built Bombes in Dayton, all sworn to secrecy) destroyed or hidden postwar; Poland's founding contribution — Rejewski's bomba — nearly erased from the record.
- **SIGSALY and Delilah** (Era 7): secure digital voice — the birth of digital audio — locked away for decades; Turing's Delilah report unreleased for ~70 years.
- **The OCR connection**: the first commercial optical character reader, GISMO (1951), was built by David Shepard — a cryptanalyst at the NSA's predecessor agency, teaching machines to *read* text after days spent teaching them to *break* it. Machine reading and machine codebreaking grew from the same soil, and Turing-era pattern-recognition and statistical methods fed both.
- **D-Day by tide machine**: Doodson computes Normandy's tides for a site he knows only as "Position Z" — computation on a need-to-know basis (Era 7).
- **Bombsight oaths**: Norden bombardiers swore to defend the device's secrecy with their lives; naval fire-control mechanisms were crown jewels of state.
- **Nuclear computing**: ENIAC's first job, MANIAC, the Monte Carlo method — all born classified; Los Alamos ran on rooms of human computers before the machines arrived.
- **Venona**: US decryption of Soviet cables, worked from 1943, kept secret until **1995** — a fifty-year computation in a vault.
- **GCHQ's stolen thunder**: public-key cryptography invented in secret, 1970–74; unrevealed until 1997 — the inventors watched the world celebrate others for their own discovery, and said nothing.
- **NSA and the industry**: Harvest (1962), influence on IBM's Stretch, decades as supercomputing's quietest best customer; the DES S-box mystery — IBM had independently discovered *differential cryptanalysis* in 1974 and, at NSA's request, kept the attack secret for ~20 years while the public suspected a backdoor (the secret changes had actually *strengthened* the cipher).
- **Operation Rubicon** (revealed 2020): the CIA and BND secretly *owned* Crypto AG, selling compromised cipher machines to 100+ governments for decades — the supply chain itself as the wiretap.
- **Still secret now**: classified supercomputers absent from the TOP500 list; TEMPEST emission-security standards; cryptanalytic capabilities we can only infer. An honest card can say: *parts of this timeline are still blank, and some blanks are deliberate.*

**J3. The human cost — and the rewritten record**
- Oath-bound silence: Bletchley's ~10,000 workers, including married couples who didn't tell each other until the 1970s; ENIAC's programmers and the Navy's WAVES uncredited for decades.
- **Alan Turing**: defined computation, helped win the war — his heroism itself classified — prosecuted in 1952, dead in 1954; public apology 2009, royal pardon 2013. Secrecy meant his country punished him without knowing whom it was punishing.
- Historiography card for students: because the fastest machines were secret, the *public* history of computing was wrong for 30–50 years — and is still being declassified. Primary sources keep surfacing. This history is a computation that hasn't halted.
### Thread K — Ethnomathematics: the mathematics of the world's peoples

**K1. The field itself**
- **Ubiratan D'Ambrosio** names the discipline (1984): the mathematics of identifiable cultural groups — not only "civilizations" but carpenters, tailors, weavers, navigators, street vendors. Everyone's math counts.
- Founders' shelf: Zaslavsky's *Africa Counts* (1973); Marcia & Robert Ascher decoding quipu mathematics; Paulus Gerdes on sona and weaving geometry; Ron Eglash's *African Fractals* (1999).
- **Alan Bishop's six universal activities** — counting, locating, measuring, designing, playing, explaining: every documented culture does all six. A perfect organizing card.

**K2. Africa**
- **Fractal architecture** (Eglash): recursive, self-similar village layouts (Ba-ila), Ethiopian processional crosses, Mokoulek — scaling geometry as design tradition.
- **The binary lineage**: Ifá's 256 odu → medieval Islamic and European geomancy's 16 binary figures → (Eglash's argued thread) influences reaching Leibniz — binary divination millennia before binary machines.
- **Borana calendar** (Kenya/Ethiopia): a working lunar-stellar calendar computed by specialists without writing.
- **Ethiopian peasant multiplication**: doubling-and-halving — binary arithmetic as folk method (cross-ref H3).
- Kente, Ndebele, and mud-cloth **symmetry systems**; Gerdes on the theorems hidden inside basket-weaving; Yoruba base-20 subtractive numerals (45 said as "five from ten from three twenties").

**K3. Oceania & Australia**
- **Kinship algebra**: Warlpiri "skin name" marriage systems form genuine finite groups (the Aschers' analysis: a dihedral group of order 8) — abstract algebra lived as social structure.
- **Songlines**: routes across a continent stored and retrieved as song — geography as computed, memorized sequence.
- Māori **kōwhaiwhai** rafter patterns and **tukutuku** lattice panels: systematic symmetry classes; Pacific navigation and PNG body-counting (cross-ref Thread F).

**K4. The Americas**
- Aztec **Codex Vergara** surveying (Era 4): field areas via indigenous algorithms and fractional units — colonial-era math running parallel to Europe's.
- Maya time-mathematics, quipu & yupana (cross-refs); **Navajo weaving symmetry** and sandpainting geometry; **wampum** belts as treaty records — data woven in shell.
- **Kaktovik numerals (1994)**: schoolchildren inventing a numeral system that reaches Unicode — ethnomathematics happening *now* (Era 9).

**K5. Asia**
- **Kolam / rangoli** (Tamil Nadu and beyond): dot-grid, single-line threshold drawings executed from memory each dawn — formally studied by computer scientists as picture languages and array grammars; algorithms performed as devotion, mostly by women.
- **Sangaku & wasan** (Era 4): geometry as temple offering; Seki's determinants.
- **Girih tiles** (Era 3); **Balinese pawukon calendar**: ten concurrent week-lengths running simultaneously — daily life scheduled by least-common-multiple.

**K6. European folk mathematics**
- **Yan tan tethera**: Brythonic base-20 sheep-scoring chants surviving among shepherds into the 20th century — a numeral system preserved as work-song.
- **Split tally sticks** as unforgeable contracts (and the 1834 Parliament fire, Era 5); **knitting and lace patterns as executable code** — knitters "read" compact instruction languages; wartime knitting was even used to smuggle coded messages (cross-ref Thread J).
- **Change ringing** (Era 4): permutation theory in the belfry; quilt-block geometry and its symmetry groups.

**K7. Sound & rhythm as computation**
- **Talking drums** (West/Central Africa): two-tone transmission of full phrases using tonal-language redundancy — long-distance messaging whose information-theoretic cleverness stunned later engineers.
- **Indian tala** cycles and konnakol spoken rhythm: recursive subdivision performed at speed.
- **Euclidean rhythms** (Toussaint, 2004): a huge share of world rhythm patterns are generated by Euclid's GCD algorithm — a 2,300-year-old algorithm, dancing.

**K8. Street mathematics (the pedagogy bombshell)**
- Nunes, Carraher & Schliemann, Brazil (1985–93): child street vendors ~98% accurate on transaction arithmetic — and ~37% on the *same problems* posed as school-style written exercises.
- Card thesis: mathematics lives in context and purpose; schooling that strips both can strip the competence too. Bridges directly into Thread L.
- Closing card: "ethnomathematics" is not math for *other* people. Our own school mathematics — the 1893 sequence, the standard algorithms — is also somebody's cultural tradition (see Thread L). All mathematics is somebody's ethnomathematics.

### Thread L — How We Came to Teach Math This Way (the lag, and the orphaned history)

**L1. The 4,000-year classroom**
- **The edubba** (c. 2000 BCE): clay-tablet homework, table-copying, teacher's model on one side and student's shaky copy on the other — the problem set is as old as writing.
- Scribal schools (Egypt, Babylon): worked examples, rote-plus-variation — recognizably modern pedagogy.
- Greece: the **quadrivium** (arithmetic, geometry, music, astronomy); Plato's "let no one ignorant of geometry enter"; **Euclid as the textbook for ~2,200 years** — no book but scripture has taught longer.
- Medieval universities: computus and *oral* disputation — for most of history, math was examined by talking, not writing.
- The two-track split: university mathematics for the learned; **abbaco schools** (Era 3) for merchants' children — a practical/academic divide that still structures curricula.
- **Vernacular revolution**: Adam Ries (1522), Robert Recorde (1557) — cheap printed textbooks in living languages; mass numeracy becomes possible.
- **The exam is invented**: the Cambridge Tripos (18th–19th c.) creates the written, timed, ranked mathematics examination — and instantly, the coaching industry, cramming, and teach-to-the-test. Modern assessment is a ~250-year-old technology with known side effects.
- **The Prussian machine** (1810s–40s): age grades, state curriculum, normal schools; the blackboard (c. 1801). The delivery system we still use predates the telegraph.
- **The freeze**: the Committee of Ten (1893) fixes arithmetic → algebra → geometry → trig; with calculus added (1955), that is *still* the standard walk — designed for 1890s college prep, not for a world that runs on statistics, linear algebra, and algorithms.

**L2. The reform pendulum**
- Klein's Meran reforms (1905) push functions and calculus into European schools; the US largely declines.
- **New Math** (1957–58, post-Sputnik): set theory handed to teachers who had never studied it — the definitive national-scale demonstration of the training gap; collapsed by the early 1970s (*Why Johnny Can't Add*, 1973; Tom Lehrer's satire in between).
- Back-to-basics (1970s) → calculator wars (1975 onward) → NCTM standards (1989) → **Math Wars** (1990s) → high-stakes testing (2001) → **Common Core** (2010), where parents' inability to help with homework put the generational gap on every kitchen table → computer-based-math movement (2010s).
- Pattern card: the pendulum swings roughly every 15–20 years — about one teacher-generation. That is not a coincidence; it's the lag mechanism oscillating.

**L3. The lag engine (core thesis card-set)**
- **The arithmetic of the gap**: a teacher certified in year X teaches until ~X+35. Their methods were largely absorbed not in training but as a *student* around X−15 — Lortie's "apprenticeship of observation" (1975): ~13,000 hours of watching teachers before training even begins. Their textbooks were written by authors trained around X−40. So classroom content routinely trails the frontier by 50–100 years — *before* compounding.
- **Compounding card**: each generation can only pass on what it received plus the little it retrained on; whatever a generation misses, the next generation cannot inherit. Gaps don't just persist — they *accumulate*, era over era, like the curriculum's own dark matter.
- **Klein's double discontinuity** (1908): future teachers experience university math as irrelevant to school math, then return to classrooms and teach school math untouched by university math — the loop that locks the lag in place.
- **Case files**: Calculus (1687) → standard high-school course in 1955: **~270 years**. Statistics (Fisher, 1920s) → still marginal in K-12 a century on. Computer science (1936–1950s) → still not universal after ~80 years. Linear algebra — the actual arithmetic of AI (Thread A) — effectively absent from school. Log tables were still being taught *after* the HP-35 made them obsolete.
- **Curriculum as geology**: every stratum was deposited by a dead era's needs — navigation left trigonometry, commerce left arithmetic drill, the Cold War left New Math's residue, the testing era left procedure-first habits. Little is ever removed. The sediment *is* the syllabus. Students deserve to be told which layer they're standing on.

**L4. The orphaned history (the site's reason to exist)**
- **Too old for computing class**: a programming course can't spare a week for Babbage, never mind Sumer; "history of computing" is one slide on day one.
- **Too new for history class**: school history runs out of steam around 1945–1990; the eighty most computationally transformative years in the human story belong to *no* course, no teacher, no exam.
- So the lineage is kept by museums, retro-computing hobbyists, and documentaries — while students carry 10¹²-FLOP/s devices with zero formal knowledge of where they came from.
- Mathematics' own history is orphaned the same way: a student can finish calculus without hearing a single date, and even the names are wrong ("Gaussian" elimination appears in the *Nine Chapters*, ~2,000 years before Gauss).
- **Mission card**: this site lives deliberately in that gap — the history too old for CS and too new for History — built for the students who need it most: the ones practicing, by hand, the very operations machines now perform 10¹⁸ times per second.

**L5. Cross-links**: Thread E (why learn it by hand) · H2 (anzan — training that demonstrably works) · K8 (street math — context carries competence) · Thread A (what the curriculum hasn't caught up to) · Thread J (why even the recent history went untaught: much of it was classified).

### Thread M — The Signal: a history of communication technology

**M1. Signals before electricity**
- **Sound over distance**: talking drums (Thread K7), signal horns, church bells, and **whistled languages** — Silbo Gomero in the Canary Islands compresses Spanish into whistles that carry across whole valleys, still taught in schools there today.
- **Light and smoke**: beacon chains from Aeschylus' Troy relay to the Great Wall's smoke towers to England's Armada beacons (1588) — one bit ("they're coming"), delivered at the speed of sight.
- **Relay infrastructure**: Persia's Royal Road couriers (Herodotus' "neither snow nor rain…" describes them), Rome's cursus publicus, and the Inca **chasqui** runners — who carried *quipus*: a data network transporting data structures (Thread F).
- **Carrier pigeons**: from ancient Egypt to Reuters' founding pigeon fleet (1850) to WWII medal-winning birds — packets with wings.
- **The Chappe optical telegraph** (1794, Era 4): a mechanical national network complete with protocols, acknowledgments, flow control, encryption, and — by 1834 — its first hacker exploit. "The mechanical internet," half a century before Morse.
- **Heliographs and flag semaphore**: mirror-flash telegraphy served armies into the 20th century; naval flag codes ran fleets.

**M2. The wire**
- **The Victorian Internet** (Standage's framing): the electric telegraph brings news tickers, wire fraud, long-distance romances, and even weddings conducted by telegraph — every social phenomenon of the internet, rehearsed in the 1800s.
- **Codes as economics**: per-word cable pricing spawns commercial codebooks compressing whole sentences into single words — compression, invented by accountants.
- **Cable mathematics**: the 1858 transatlantic cable dies of over-voltage ignorance; Kelvin's analysis and mirror galvanometer save 1866 — signal theory learned the hard way, by ocean.
- **Pneumatic tube networks** under Paris, London, Berlin, New York: physical packet delivery, some running into the 1980s.
- **The paper-tape lineage**: Baudot's 5-bit code → teleprinters → telex — and punched tape becomes early computer I/O; Colossus read enciphered teleprinter tape at 5,000 characters/second (Threads G, J). Communication gear and computers share one bloodstream.
- **The telephone era**: Bell (1876), exchanges, Strowger's dial (1891), and generations of switchboard operators — human packet routers (Thread B) — automated away exactly as the human computers would be.
- **Fax before phones**: Bain (1843) and Caselli's pantelegraph in commercial service by 1865 — image transmission is older than voice transmission.

**M3. Through the air**
- **The photophone** (Bell, 1880): voice carried on a sunbeam — Bell called it his greatest invention; it waited a century for fiber optics to vindicate it.
- **Radio**: Hertz's laboratory waves → Marconi's transatlantic "S" (1901) → broadcasting (KDKA, 1920) → radar in WWII, which poured money into the electronics and mathematics that built the computer industry.
- **Satellites**: Sputnik's beep (1957) → Telstar's live TV (1962) → GPS: atomic-clock time signals turned into position by relativistic computation — Einstein running silently in every phone.

**M4. The digital merger**
- **Shannon (1948)**: one theory for every channel — drum, wire, light, radio — with the *bit* as universal currency. Communication and computation formally become a single subject.
- **Error correction**: Hamming (1950), Reed–Solomon (1960), and their descendants in every phone and spacecraft — arithmetic that heals damaged messages mid-flight.
- **Packet switching → ARPANET → TCP/IP → the Web** (Eras 8–9): the network becomes a computer; the computer becomes a network.
- **Fiber**: Kao's 1966 insight that glass could carry light for kilometers; TAT-8 (1988) crosses the Atlantic; today undersea fiber carries ~99% of intercontinental data along routes eerily echoing the 1866 cable map.
- **Convergence**: the smartphone is telegraph + telephone + radio + television + camera + library + computer — every channel in this thread, fused into a slab.

**M5. Theme cards**
- **Twins, not neighbors**: communication and computation are both transformations of encoded symbols. Every era's signaling system needed codes, and every code invited computation — Morse's compression, cable codebooks, Baudot's binary, Shannon's unification.
- **Numbers travel first**: each new fastest channel carried prices, odds, tides, and war before it carried anything else.
- **The latency ladder** (companion to Thread A's speed ladder): courier (days) → beacon (minutes, one bit) → Chappe (minutes, low bandwidth) → telegraph (seconds) → light in fiber (milliseconds) — and today the speed of light itself is the wall: high-frequency traders build microwave towers because fiber's path is microseconds too *curved*. Latency, not arithmetic, is now often the scarce resource.
- Pedagogy card: a student who understands Morse's short-E and long-Q already understands the core idea of data compression — 19th-century hand-skills as a royal road into information theory.

### Thread N — When Computation Fails
- **The opening card is Babbage's motive**: he built engines *because human tables were full of errors* — this whole subject begins with failure.
- **Therac-25 (1985–87)**: race conditions in a radiation-therapy machine kill patients — software safety engineering is born of tragedy.
- **Patriot missile (1991)**: 0.1 seconds has no exact binary representation; 100 hours of clock drift = a 687-meter miss and 28 dead soldiers. Floating point as life and death (see IEEE 754, Era 9).
- **Pentium FDIV (1994)**: a few missing lookup-table entries; discovered by a mathematician (Thomas Nicely) cross-checking prime computations — caught because a human was *verifying by independent calculation*. A $475M recall.
- **Ariane 5 (1996)**: a 64-bit float jammed into a 16-bit integer; reused code from a slower rocket; $370M of fireworks in 37 seconds.
- **Mars Climate Orbiter (1999)**: one team used pound-seconds, the other newton-seconds — Thread F's "number as quantity" lesson, fatal at Mars. Units are not decoration.
- **Y2K (2000)**: two-digit years meet the millennium; a massive global remediation *works*, so smoothly that people now call it a hoax — the "successful prevention looks like overreaction" card.
- **Reinhart–Rogoff (2013)**: an Excel range error (plus contestable choices) in a paper that had steered global austerity policy — found by a graduate student who *redid the arithmetic*. The site's best modern argument for checking by hand.
- **Lorenz (1963)**: chaos discovered through rounding — some systems have mathematical forecast horizons no supercomputer can push past.
- **Gödel & the halting problem**: some questions no computer can ever decide — failure elevated to theorem.
- **Closing card**: machines compute wrong at exactly the same speed they compute right. Estimation, unit sense, and sanity checks — the skills of hand arithmetic — are the human firmware that catches the machine (Glenn trusted Johnson, not the computer; Nicely caught Intel).

### Thread O — Memory: How Machines Remember
- **Before machines**: clay tablets, quipu knots, tally sticks, books, card catalogs — every era's storage (cross-refs Eras 1–3, Thread S).
- **Bits as echoes**: mercury delay lines store numbers as circulating sound pulses (EDSAC); Williams tubes store them as glowing spots on a CRT — memory you could *hear* and *see*.
- **Core memory (1950s–70s)**: thousands of ferrite rings, each threaded by hand — for two decades, nearly every bit on Earth passed through the fingers of women doing precision textile work.
- **Core rope & Apollo**: program memory physically *woven* — wire through a core = 1, around it = 0; NASA engineers called it "LOL memory" (little old ladies). Software you could hold, made by weavers (Threads B, Q; Jacquard's descendants).
- **The magnetic era**: drum → tape → RAMAC's 5 MB ton (1956) → floppy (1971) → the hard-drive density explosion.
- **Optical & solid state**: CD (1982, with Reed–Solomon armor — Thread M), flash memory (Masuoka, 1987), SSDs; experimental DNA storage — data written back into molecules, where Era 1's tokens began.
- **The price-of-a-bit ladder** (companion to Thread A): from dollars per bit to trillions of bits per dollar — memory's own vertiginous curve, worth its own comparison card.
- **Theme card**: storage has always been embodied labor — scribes, knotters, weavers, threaders — until suddenly it wasn't; remembering *how* memory was made keeps the abstraction honest.

### Thread P — The Data Thread: Probability, Statistics & Seeing Numbers
- **Born at the gambling table and the plague ledger**: Pascal–Fermat (1654) make chance calculable; Graunt (1662) reads London's death rolls as *data*; Halley (1693) prices annuities — the state and commerce learn to compute risk.
- **Bayes and Laplace**: inverse probability — updating belief by arithmetic; Laplace computes planetary masses with it, and modern AI is soaked in it.
- **Gauss**: least squares and the normal curve — error itself becomes lawful (Ceres, Era 5).
- **The picture-makers**: Playfair invents the chart (1786); Nightingale weaponizes it (1858); Minard's Napoleon-in-Russia map — often called the finest graphic ever drawn; Tukey's exploratory data analysis; today's dashboards.
- **The uncomfortable card**: Galton and Pearson built correlation and regression in the service of eugenics; Fisher too was entangled. Methods outlive motives — users must supply the ethics. Taught honestly, this is one of the site's most important cards.
- **"Statistics" contains "state"**: censuses from Babylon to Hollerith (1890) to modern "big data" — the same problem at 10¹⁵ scale.
- **Closing card**: statistics is the mathematics most adults actually need, and Thread L explains why school still teaches so little of it.

### Thread Q — Labor & Automation: Who Does the Work?
- **The Turk, twice**: Kempelen's 1770 hoax hid a human inside the machine; Amazon's Mechanical Turk (2005) and today's global data-labeling workforce hide thousands of humans inside "AI." Same card, 250 years apart — always ask who's inside the box.
- **The Luddites, corrected** (1811): machine-breaking as collective bargaining by other means, not technophobia — the most slandered workers in industrial history.
- **The computing proletariat**: de Prony's pyramid → table factories → typing pools → comptometer rooms → "computer" quietly ceasing to be a job title (1950s–60s) → switchboard automation (Strowger to direct dial) → phototypesetting ending hot-metal careers → the ATM paradox (teller employment *rose* for decades as cheap branches multiplied — automation reshapes rather than simply deletes).
- **The contests as ritual**: John Henry vs. the steam drill; the 1946 soroban vs. electric calculator match; Kasparov (1997); Lee Sedol (2016) — cultures stage man-vs-machine duels at every threshold, and the site's students are living through the next one.
- **Honest closing card**: automation transitions are real, painful, uneven — and navigable. Every generation on this timeline adapted; understanding the machine, rather than merely operating or fearing it, has always been the strongest position. That is what this site is for.

### Thread R — Commerce as Computation
- **Writing was invented by accountants**: Mesopotamian tokens → clay envelopes → cuneiform (Era 1) — literacy itself is a spinoff of inventory.
- **Pacioli's mirror (1494)**: double-entry bookkeeping — debits equal credits, a checksum you can run by candlelight; Goethe called it one of the finest inventions of the human mind.
- **The infrastructure of trust**: bills of exchange, interest tables, ready reckoners (Thread H4), actuarial tables (Thread P), the Medici network moving value as *messages* (Thread M).
- **Speed is money**: Reuters' pigeons, the Blanc brothers' telegraph hack (1834), the ticker tape (1867), and today's microwave HFT links (Thread M5) — finance has always bought the fastest channel first.
- **The retail stack**: cash registers → the barcode's first beep (1974, a pack of Wrigley's gum) → credit-card networks authorizing billions of transactions → the ATM (1967) → VisiCalc putting a ledger in every office (Era 9).
- **The tally stick reborn**: blockchain (2008) — distributed, unforgeable, split-record ledgers; Era 5's burned sticks would recognize the idea instantly.
- **Theme card**: from clay tokens to high-frequency trading, commerce has been computation's most reliable paymaster — most machines on this timeline were bought to count money.

### Thread S — The Card and the Link: From Library to HyperCard
- **Alexandria's Pinakes** (Era 2): the catalog is older than almost everything it catalogs.
- **Monks as search engines**: concordances and indexes compiled by hand — years of labor to let a reader "query" scripture; the florilegium as a curated feed.
- **Cards take over**: Gesner's paper slips (1540s); revolutionary France catalogs seized libraries on the backs of playing cards (1790s); the card catalog and Dewey standardize retrieval.
- **Otlet's Mundaneum** (1910): 12 million cards, a query-by-mail service, and a 1934 vision of screens networked to a universal repository — a paper internet, dreamed in Brussels.
- **The lineage of the dream**: Bush's Memex (1945) → Licklider's "Man-Computer Symbiosis" (1960) → Engelbart's Demo (1968) → Nelson's hypertext and Xanadu (1965) → PLATO's community (1960s–70s) → Kay's Dynabook → PARC's Alto → Macintosh (1984).
- **HyperCard (1987)**: Atkinson's "software erector set" — teachers, artists, and kids building linked-card stacks without "programming"; *Myst* was built in it; Ward Cunningham designed the first wiki as "HyperCard for the web"; countless classrooms made their first software in it.
- **The Web (1991)**: hypertext's global victory — and a card on what was lost (two-way links, Xanadu's transclusion and micropayment dreams).
- **Mission card**: this site is deliberately built as a HyperCard-style stack. Its *form* is an artifact from its own timeline — students navigate the history of computation using a beloved piece of it.

### Thread T — The Harmony Thread: music, myth & the mathematical universe

**T1. The music of the spheres**
- **The monochord** (Era 2): Pythagorean legend — hammers in a smithy, then a single string divided — reveals that consonance lives in small whole-number ratios; the first physical law expressed numerically, and the founding *proof* that nature obeys mathematics.
- **Musica universalis**: Plato's *Timaeus* tunes the world-soul; Cicero's *Dream of Scipio* lets Scipio *hear* the planets; Boethius (Era 3) transmits the doctrine and plants music in the **quadrivium** — for a millennium, the educated studied music as a branch of mathematics, between arithmetic and astronomy.
- **Kepler's wager (1619)**: if the heavens are harmonic, their chords can be computed — and inside *Harmonices Mundi*, amid planetary scales and angelic choirs, sits the third law of planetary motion. Mysticism, methodically pursued, delivered mechanics.
- **The metaphor that never died**: modern physics still speaks of vibrating strings, resonant modes, and harmonics — from spectroscopy to quantum mechanics, the monochord's vocabulary runs straight through science.

**T2. Mersenne: the friar who counted sound**
- ***Harmonie universelle* (1636)**: ~1,500 pages measuring everything musical — construction, dimensions, stringing, and technique for lutes, viols, violins, keyboards, winds, drums, and bells, so precisely recorded that modern luthiers still reconstruct 17th-century instruments from it.
- **The string laws**: vary one thing at a time — length, tension (weights hung from strings), thickness, material (gut, brass, even gold wire) — yielding frequency ∝ (1/L)·√(T/μ). The conceptual leap: pitch is a *rate of vibration*, a countable physical event, not an abstract proportion.
- **Counting sound itself**: strings and ropes long enough that their swings can be counted by eye against a pulse — then the laws scale those slow counts up to hundreds of vibrations per second, assigning real numbers to musical notes for the first time. This is why he's called the father of acoustics.
- **The harmonic-series puzzle**: one plucked string faintly sounds its octave, twelfth, and double octave *simultaneously* — how can one body vibrate at several rates at once? Mersenne posed the question over and over in letters; the answer (superposition of modes) took Sauveur, Daniel Bernoulli, and finally Fourier. A friar's puzzlement drove a century of mathematics.
- **Consonance as coincidence**: intervals sound sweet when their vibrations line up often — the octave every 2nd cycle, the fifth every 6th; dissonances rarely align. Not the modern account, but it moved consonance from number mysticism into physics, with intervals ranked on principle.
- **Computing equal temperament**: the octave split into 12 identical semitones of ratio ¹²√2 ≈ 1.05946 — computed via mean proportionals (logarithms were brand new) and printed as string lengths for fretting. Fretted lutes practically forced equal temperament; Mersenne's arithmetic helped legitimize the tuning that now underlies nearly all Western music.
- **The combinatorics of song**: factorials tabulated up to 64!; all **720 permutations of a six-note hexachord printed in full** across several pages; 22! melodies — sextillions — declared beyond all the world's singers. One of the first applications of combinatorics to an art, feeding straight into Leibniz's combinatorial dreams (see Llull, Era 3).
- **The human internet hub**: Mersenne's correspondence web — Descartes (whose first work, the *Compendium musicae*, was written for this circle), Galileo, Fermat, Pascal, Doni, experimenters across Europe testing echoes, resonance, sound underwater. Before journals existed, *he* was the network — and music was his test case proving nature obeys mathematical law.
- **Still in the machine**: Mersenne primes (2^p−1) drive GIMPS, the distributed search that finds nearly every record prime (Era 9), and the *Mersenne Twister* generates the random numbers inside countless simulations. Four centuries on, the friar's name is compiled into running code.

**T3. Babel and the perfect language**
- **The Tower of Babel**: the myth of one lost universal tongue — and the centuries-long scholarly quest to recover or rebuild the "Adamic language" in which words perfectly matched things.
- **The 17th-century projects**: Dalgarno's and Wilkins's constructed philosophical languages (1668) classify all of reality into a symbol system — Borges made Wilkins famous again; Leibniz turns the quest computational: a *characteristica universalis* plus a *calculus ratiocinator*, so that disputes end with "let us calculate" (Thread C; Llull, Era 3).
- **Babel answered sideways**: the perfect language never arrived for humans — it arrived for machines. Boole's algebra, Frege's notation, and finally programming languages and protocols: humanity's actual universal tongues turned out to be code and TCP/IP (Thread M).
- **Musical and constructed tongues**: Solresol (1827) — words made of do-re-mi, equally speakable, singable, playable, or whistled; Esperanto; Interlingua feeding the first machine-translation dreams (Era 8).

**T4. The language of the birds**
- **The mythic tongue**: the secret speech of birds granted to Solomon and Siegfried, the alchemists' "green language," Attar's *Conference of the Birds* (1177) — a spiritual quest structured like an algorithm, thirty birds seeking the one.
- **From myth to data**: Kircher transcribes birdsong into staff notation (1650) — animal sound becomes recordable information; his arca musarithmica composes by combinatorial lookup.
- **The modern research program**: bioacoustics and machine learning now parse whale codas, birdsong syntax, and bee dances — today's AI projects to decode animal communication are the language of the birds pursued with GPUs.
- Cross-links: whistled human languages (Thread M) and talking drums (Thread K) — people already speak in pitch.

**T5. Music as computation**
- **Notation is a program**: Guido's staff (Era 3) plots pitch against time — arguably the first graph — and a score is a set of instructions executed by performers; the player-piano roll is literally a punched program, Jacquard's first cousin; MIDI (1983) turns performance into a live data stream.
- **Algorithmic composition, four centuries deep**: Kircher's box (1650) → 18th-century musical dice games → change ringing's permutation "programs" (Era 4) → the *Illiac Suite* and MUSIC I (1957) → today's generative AI music.
- **Fourier closes Mersenne's case (1822)**: every sound is a sum of sines; with the FFT (Era 8) this becomes the arithmetic inside MP3s, Auto-Tune, speech recognition, and every phone call — your earbuds run 19th-century heat theory answering a 17th-century friar's question.
- **The hearing sciences**: Sauveur names acoustics (1701); Helmholtz dissects hearing with resonators (1863) — the ear itself revealed as a frequency analyzer.
- **Theme card**: music was the laboratory where the West first learned that number governs nature — the quadrivium's bridge from arithmetic to physics. Every audio app is downstream of a monochord, and a student practicing scales is running the oldest experiment in mathematical physics.

### Thread U — The Hermetic Thread: esotericism and the making of science

**U1. The current**
- **The Emerald Tablet and the Corpus Hermeticum**: "as above, so below" — texts attributed to Hermes Trismegistus and believed older than Moses; in 1463 Cosimo de' Medici orders Ficino to set Plato aside and translate Hermes *first*. The Renaissance reboots with magic at the front of the queue.
- **The Renaissance magi**: Pico della Mirandola weds Kabbalah to philosophy; Agrippa's *De occulta philosophia* treats number as power; Giulio Camillo builds a walk-in Memory Theatre of all knowledge and Giordano Bruno turns memory palaces into combinatorial art — information architecture in occult dress (Thread S).
- **John Dee** (Era 4): the *Monas Hieroglyphica*'s cosmic glyph and the Mathematical Preface that sold Euclid to England's navigators and artisans; the imperial adviser with the realm's greatest library also transcribed "angelic" language by scrying. One mind, both worlds, no contradiction felt.
- **1614 — the turn**: Casaubon's philology dates the Hermetica to late antiquity, the same year Napier publishes logarithms — the prestige of ancient magic starts falling just as computational power starts compounding.

**U2. Newton, the last of the magicians**
- Newton translated the **Emerald Tablet** into English with commentary and left ~a million manuscript words on alchemy — more than he wrote on physics — from a private laboratory he ran for decades.
- **The Keynes verdict (1936)**: buying Newton's hidden papers at Sotheby's, Keynes reported that Newton "was not the first of the age of reason. He was the last of the magicians."
- **The productive irony**: universal gravitation — invisible action at a distance — was attacked by mechanist critics (Leibniz among them) as an "occult quality." A mind at home with hidden forces could accept the equation his rationalist rivals gagged on. The mysticism may have *enabled* the physics.
- **Computing doomsday**: Newton applied his calculating powers to biblical chronology, concluding in one manuscript that the apocalypse would come "not before 2060" — the supreme calculator, running eschatology as arithmetic.
- **Still being decrypted**: the Chymistry of Isaac Newton project is digitizing the alchemical papers now — an archive suppressed for centuries not by classification (Thread J) but by embarrassment.

**U3. Leibniz, the binary God, and the I Ching**
- For Leibniz, **binary was theology**: 1 as God, 0 as nothing — creation *ex nihilo* re-enacted inside every number. He designed a medallion celebrating it: *omnibus ex nihilo ducendis sufficit unum* — "to derive everything from nothing, one suffices."
- **1701–03**: missionary Joachim Bouvet mails him Shao Yong's circular hexagram diagram; Leibniz recognizes the 64 figures as his binary numbers and publishes the connection in his memoir on binary arithmetic — Chinese divination and European theology meeting inside the number system that would run every computer on Earth (Era 2).
- **The Kabbalist cousin**: Abulafia's letter permutations and gematria (Era 3) belong to the same current as Llull's wheels — sacred combinatorics that Leibniz inherited for his *ars combinatoria* and universal characteristic (Thread T).

**U4. Kircher: the man who knew everything (and was wrong about most of it)**
- **Athanasius Kircher, S.J.** (1602–1680), Collegio Romano: some forty books claiming nearly every subject, a famous museum, and a continental reputation as "master of a hundred arts."
- **The media inventor**: *Ars Magna Lucis et Umbrae* (1646) popularizes the magic lantern — projected images, the ancestor of cinema and every screen (Thread M); his museum featured statues speaking through hidden voice tubes and a piped "Delphic oracle" — immersive audio, 1670s; plus the arca musarithmica composition box (Thread T) and the notorious cat piano he described (mercifully, likely never built).
- **The knowledge engines**: *Ars Magna Sciendi* (1669) rebuilds Llull's combinatorics as a general logic of discovery; *Polygraphia nova* (1663) offers a universal writing-and-cipher scheme with a translation box — Babel-reversal as hardware (Thread T; Thread G).
- **The data network**: from *Magnes* (1641) on, Kircher compiled magnetic declination, tide, and astronomical reports mailed in by Jesuit missionaries worldwide — a standing, global, crowdsourced observation network centuries before "citizen science."
- **The lucky wrongness**: his hieroglyph "translations" were mystical fantasy — but his claim that Coptic descends from ancient Egyptian was *right* and underpinned Champollion's real decipherment; his microscope study of plague victims' blood (*Scrutinium Pestis*, 1658) reported "little worms" — pointing toward germ theory for partly wrong reasons; he had himself lowered toward Vesuvius' glowing crater for *Mundus Subterraneus* (1665) — wrong about the subterranean fire-channels, right that you should go look.
- **The rival hub**: like Mersenne (Thread T), Kircher anchored a continental correspondence web; the young Leibniz wrote to him in admiration. Patron saint of interdisciplinarity — and a standing lesson that spectacular error, honestly pursued and publicly shared, can still drag knowledge forward.

**U5. Why this belongs on a computation timeline**
- **Research programs harden into science**: harmony-hunting → celestial mechanics (Kepler, Thread T); alchemy → chemistry (Boyle worked the same furnaces); perfect-language dreams → symbolic logic and code (Thread T); memory arts → information architecture (Thread S); Kabbalist and Llullian combinatorics → Leibniz → Boole → circuits.
- **Astrology fed astronomy**: Kepler cast horoscopes to pay for planet-hunting — "astrology, the foolish daughter, feeds her wise mother astronomy."
- **Secrecy's training ground**: alchemical ciphers, coded recipes, and priority-guarding anagrams (Galileo announced discoveries to Kepler as scrambled letters) rehearsed the habits that became scientific priority claims and cryptographic practice (Thread J).
- **The honest-history card**: none of this endorses magic. It corrects the textbook myth of pure rationalists. The founders were whole people chasing hidden order everywhere — and some of those hunts found mathematics. Motivation and method can be separated only in hindsight; today's "of course" was once somebody's heresy.
- **The Yates card**: 20th-century scholarship (Frances Yates and successors) recovered this suppressed lineage. Like Colossus (Thread J), part of science's own history was long left out of the story — this time from embarrassment rather than classification. History is a computation that hasn't halted (Thread L).
